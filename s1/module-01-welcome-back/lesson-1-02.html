LESSON 1.02 · THE ONE IDEA BEHIND EVERY VERB
The verb changes to match who is doing it
No new verbs today — just the pattern that makes the whole year make sense, shown with verbs you already own.
💡 TODAY'S BIG IDEA
A verb doesn't stay the same. Its ending shifts to tell you who is doing the action — that shift is called conjugation.

You already do this in English without noticing: you say “I am” but “she is” — same verb, different form for a different person. Spanish just does it more often, and more clearly. Once you see the pattern, every verb this year works the same way.

🔄 YOU ALREADY DO THIS IN ENGLISH

Look at the verb “to be” in English. One verb — but the word changes depending on the subject:

  • I am · you are · she is · we are · they are

Nobody taught you a rule for that — your ear just knows “I is” sounds wrong. Spanish works the same way: the ending of the verb tells you who the subject is. That is all conjugation means.

👥 FIRST · THE SUBJECTS (WHO IS DOING IT)

Every conjugation answers one question: who? These are the subject pronouns — the “who” words. You met them last year; here they are all together:

PersonSpanishEnglish
1st singularyoI
2nd singulartúyou (a friend)
3rd singularusted / él / ellayou (respectful) / he / she
1st pluralnosotros / nosotraswe
3rd pluralustedesyou all
3rd pluralellos / ellasthey

A note on register: in most of Latin America, ustedes is how you say “you all” to any group — friends, family, strangers, everyone. That is the form this course uses throughout. (You may see vosotros in movies from Spain; we will not use it here.)

📚 SEE IT · SER (TO BE) ACROSS ALL SIX SUBJECTS

Here is ser — a verb you already use. Watch the ending change for each subject. That is conjugation, live:

SubjectForm of SERMeaning
yosoyI am
túeresyou are
usted / él / ellaesyou are / he is / she is
nosotros / nosotrassomoswe are
ustedessonyou all are
ellos / ellassonthey are

Same verb, six shapes. You are not memorizing anything new — you already say yo soy and nosotros somos. Today you are just seeing why they look different.

📚 AND AGAIN · ESTAR (TO BE)

The other “to be” verb, estar, does the exact same thing — its ending matches the subject too:

SubjectForm of ESTARMeaning
yoestoyI am
túestásyou are
usted / él / ellaestáyou are / he is / she is
nosotros / nosotrasestamoswe are
ustedesestányou all are
ellos / ellasestánthey are

Notice the pattern already peeking through: the -mos ending always means “we,” and the -n ending always means a plural “they / you all.” Hold onto that — it is the same across almost every verb you will meet this year.
