LESSON 16.04 · EN EL CONSULTORIO
Una visita al médico — a visit to the doctor
Everything in this scene uses words and patterns you already own — all in the present tense.
👋 BEFORE YOU READ

Here's Carlos, who isn't feeling well today. Read the scene one line at a time. Press ▶ to hear each Spanish sentence, then read the English to check yourself. Watch doler, tener, and estar all doing their jobs.

📚 LEE Y ESCUCHA · READ & LISTEN
Carlos está enfermo hoy. — Carlos is sick today.
Le duele la cabeza y tiene fiebre. — His head hurts and he has a fever.
Por la tarde, Carlos va al médico. — In the afternoon, Carlos goes to the doctor.
El médico pregunta: «¿Qué te duele?» — The doctor asks: “What hurts?”
Carlos contesta: «Me duele el estómago y me duelen las piernas.» — Carlos answers: “My stomach hurts and my legs hurt.”
El médico dice que Carlos tiene gripe. — The doctor says that Carlos has the flu.
Ahora Carlos descansa en casa. — Now Carlos rests at home.

Notice line 5: me duele el estómago (one thing) but me duelen las piernas (two legs). Same rule as always.

🌍 SPANISH IN YOUR WORLD · MEDICAL WORDS YOU ALREADY KNOW

Here's a comforting fact: a huge amount of "medical Spanish" is made of cognates — words that look and mean almost the same in both languages. If you ever need help, you already understand a lot:

  • el hospital → hospital cognate
  • el doctor / la doctora → doctor cognate (another word for el médico)
  • la ambulancia → ambulance cognate
  • la farmacia → pharmacy (the drugstore) cognate

Across the Spanish-speaking world, the farmacia is often the first stop when you feel a little sick — the pharmacist can suggest simple remedies before you ever see a médico. Knowing these four words means you could ask for help almost anywhere.
