LESSON 12.01 · ONE WORD, TWO VERBS
In English you “know” everything. In Spanish, it depends.
Nothing to memorize yet — just meet the puzzle and see why Spanish splits “to know” in two.
👋 TODAY'S BIG IDEA
English uses one little word, “know,” for two very different things. Spanish gives each one its own verb.

Think about these three English sentences: “I know the answer.” “I know how to swim.” “I know your cousin.” In English, one word — know — covers a fact, a skill, and a person. Spanish hears those as two different ideas and uses two different verbs: saber and conocer. This week you learn which is which — and one small extra word that shows up whenever you “know” a person.

🔄 FIRST · WHAT YOU ALREADY OWN

You are ready for this. Two things you already do will make today easy:

tengo, hago, pongo, salgo — irregular yo forms lo / la / los / las — direct-object pronouns

Back in Module 4 you met verbs with a bumpy yo (I) form — like tener → tengo and hacer → hago. Both of this week's verbs have exactly that kind of surprise in the yo form, so you already know the trick: the “I” form is worth a second look, and the rest behave. And last module's direct-object pronouns remind us that Spanish cares a lot about who or what receives the action — which is exactly why the “person” rule this week matters.

🎯 BY THE END OF THIS WEEK YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
choose between saber and conocer for facts, skills, people, and places.
2

3

💡 A FIRST, GENTLE LOOK

Here is the whole idea in two sentences. Read them out loud — you'll build the details all week:

  • Sé la respuesta. — I know the answer.  (a fact → saber)
  • Conozco a Ana. — I know Ana.  (a person → conocer, plus that little a)

Facts and know-how lean on saber. People and places lean on conocer. Don't worry about memorizing yet — that's tomorrow. Today, just feel the split.

★ A WORD ON HONESTY

There's a quiet kind of honesty in language, too: saying what you actually mean. Spanish makes you pause and ask, “Do I know a fact, or am I acquainted with someone?” That habit of choosing your words with care — not just grabbing the first one — is worth carrying into everything you write and say.
