📖 READING · EN EL RESTAURANTE

Here is a short scene at a restaurant. Every verb is one you already know, and every food word is from this week. Press ▶ to hear each sentence, then read it again on your own:

La familia entra en el restaurante y mira el menú. — The family goes into the restaurant and looks at the menu.
El camarero pregunta qué quieren comer. — The waiter asks what they want to eat.
Yo pido una ensalada con tomate y manzana. — I order a salad with tomato and apple.
Mi hermano prefiere el plátano de postre. — My brother prefers the banana for dessert.
Al final, nosotros pedimos la cuenta y decimos «gracias». — In the end, we ask for the bill and say “thank you.”

Did you catch the boot verbs at work? quieren (querer), pido and pedimos (pedir), prefiere (preferir) — all review, all present tense.

🌐 SPANISH IN YOUR WORLD · THE FOOD ON YOUR PLATE

Some of the most everyday English food words are actually Spanish words that English borrowed whole:

taco → taco  ·  salsa → salsa  ·  burrito → burrito

But here is the surprising part. A few of these words did not start out as Spanish at all. When Spanish speakers first reached what is now Mexico, they met the Nahuatl language — the language of the Aztec (Mexica) people. Spanish borrowed words from Nahuatl, and English later borrowed them from Spanish. So the trail runs Nahuatl → Spanish → English:

NAHUATL chocolātl → chocolate → chocolate
NAHUATL tomatl → tomate → tomato
NAHUATL āhuacatl → aguacate → avocado

Give credit where it's due: chocolate, tomato, and avocado are Nahuatl in origin — they passed through Spanish on their way into English, but they were not Spanish words to begin with. Every time you say them, you're speaking three languages at once.
