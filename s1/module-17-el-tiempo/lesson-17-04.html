The weather decides the plan
Read how the sky shapes what people are going to do — then two surprises about seasons and words.
📖 READ & HEAR · EL TIEMPO Y LOS PLANES

Every sentence links the weather to a plan with ir a + infinitive — the "going to" pattern you already own. Read each line, then press ▶ to hear it and say it back.

Hoy hace sol y hace buen tiempo. — Today it's sunny and the weather is nice.
Por eso, voy a caminar en el parque con mis amigos. — So I'm going to walk in the park with my friends.
Mañana va a llover, entonces voy a leer en casa. — Tomorrow it's going to rain, so I'm going to read at home.
En el invierno hace mucho frío y a veces nieva. — In winter it's very cold and sometimes it snows.
Cuando nieva, mi familia y yo vamos a preparar chocolate caliente. — When it snows, my family and I are going to make hot chocolate.
En el verano hace calor. ¿Qué vas a hacer tú? — In the summer it's hot. What are you going to do?

🌐 SPANISH IN YOUR WORLD · FLIPPED SEASONS & BORROWED WORDS

1. The seasons are flipped south of the equator. When it's December and winter in the United States, it's summer in Argentina and Chile — countries in the Southern Hemisphere have their seasons reversed. So while you might sing about a cold December, families in Buenos Aires or Santiago are heading to the beach. Same month, opposite season.

2. Two English weather words came from Spanish. When English needed words for violent storms, it borrowed them:

tornado→ English tornado (borrowed from Spanish)
huracán→ English hurricane

Here's the honest part of the story: huracán did not begin as a Spanish word. Spanish itself borrowed it from Taíno, an Indigenous language of the Caribbean, and then English borrowed it from Spanish. So "hurricane" traveled Taíno → Spanish → English — a good reminder that words carry history, and that Indigenous languages shaped the words we use every day.

💡 THE BIG PICTURE

Weather isn't just words — it's a window into a place. Half the world flips its seasons, and some of our stormiest English words are gifts from Spanish and the Taíno people. When you talk about the weather in Spanish, you're joining a very old, very global conversation.
